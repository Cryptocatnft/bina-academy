Hallucinations and How to Catch Them

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Know why AI models invent facts, which tasks are high-risk, and build a verification habit that catches errors before your boss or client does.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Why models invent things

A model's job is producing plausible text, not true text. When it hits a gap in its knowledge, it doesn't say "I don't know" unless trained and prompted to — it fills the gap with something *statistically shaped like* an answer. A fake citation looks exactly like a real one: right author cadence, right year range, right journal vibe. Hallucinations have decreased with each model generation and with reasoning models, but they have not disappeared — and the confident tone never changes. Never mistake fluency for accuracy.

The high-risk categories

Some claims deserve automatic suspicion:

  • Citations and URLs — invented papers and dead links are the classic hallucination.
  • Numbers and statistics — "73% of users" is easy to generate, hard to have actually measured.
  • Names, dates, legal and medical specifics — especially for people or events after the training cutoff.
  • Tools and APIs that may not exist — models happily invent configuration options and function parameters.

Rule of thumb: the more a claim *costs you if wrong*, the more you verify. Hallucinated haiku: harmless. Hallucinated contract clause: you're fired.

The verification workflow

Professionals don't hope — they check, proportionally to the stakes:

1. Ask for sources and confidence. "Cite the source for each claim; mark any claim you are unsure about with [VERIFY]."

2. Spot-check. Verify 2-3 specific facts independently (web search, the original document). If they fail, treat the whole output as suspect.

3. Cross-examine. "You stated X. Give me your evidence, and tell me how you could be wrong."

4. Separate generation from validation. Use a second AI pass — or a different model — to review the first: "Find every unverifiable or likely-false claim in this text."

5. Human sign-off for anything legal, medical, financial, or public-facing.

Designing hallucination-resistant prompts

Prevention beats detection. Strong habits:

  • Ground the model: paste the actual source document and say "answer only from this text; if it's not there, say so."
  • Allow the escape hatch: explicitly permit "I don't know" — models hallucinate most when cornered into answering.
  • Ask for structure with an unknown option: outputs where every field is either sourced or marked unknown.

In interviews and on the job, saying "here's my verification workflow" signals seniority far more than claiming "the AI is always right" — which, honestly, nobody believes anymore.

Copy-ready prompt

Answer only from the text below. If the answer is not in the text, reply exactly: "Not found in the provided text." Cite the paragraph you used for each answer. [PASTE TEXT]

Review this AI-generated answer for likely hallucinations. List each risky claim, rate it low/medium/high risk, and tell me exactly how to verify it: [PASTE ANSWER]. Do not rewrite the answer.

💪 Exercise

Ask an AI chat: "Give me 5 recent statistics about remote work, with sources." Verify each source actually exists and says what's claimed. Log the results: how many were real, partially real, or fabricated. This 20-minute drill is the single best hallucination training there is.
