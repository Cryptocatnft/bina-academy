Context Windows and Why AI Forgets

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Understand tokens and context windows, and learn the professional techniques — summarize, compress, retrieve — that keep long conversations and big documents from falling apart.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Tokens: the unit of memory

Models don't read words — they read tokens, chunks of text roughly three-quarters of a word in English (Hebrew usually costs more tokens per word). The context window is everything the model can 'see' at once: your instructions, the conversation history, pasted documents, and its own previous answers. In 2026, windows range from about 128K tokens (roughly a 300-page book) to over a million on some models. Sounds huge — but everything you *add* eats space, every reply accumulates, and long chats die by a thousand crumbs. API pricing is also per token, so wasted context is wasted money.

Lost in the middle

The catch nobody puts on the marketing page: models are strongest with information at the beginning and end of their context, and noticeably weaker with the middle. Stuff 200 pages into a model and ask about page 97, and quality drops — sometimes badly. Bigger windows made 'just paste it all in' *possible*; they did not make it *good*. Professionals treat the context window like a whiteboard: put the important stuff at the top, keep it tidy, and erase what you no longer need. This is also why naive 'chat with your PDF' apps disappoint — the good ones use retrieval, which you'll build yourself in Lesson 9.

Three techniques that fix forgetting

1. Summarize and restart. When a long chat drifts, ask: *'Summarize our decisions, constraints, and open questions in a compact block.'* Start a fresh chat and paste that block. You keep the knowledge, drop the noise.

2. Compress with standing instructions. Keep a short, fixed block — goals, format rules, key facts — at the top of every chat, and reference it instead of re-explaining.

3. Retrieve instead of remembering. For large document collections, don't paste everything — fetch only the relevant chunks per question. That's RAG (retrieval-augmented generation), and it's 26% of agentic job postings. You build one from scratch in Lesson 9.

Summarize, compress, retrieve: three words to remember.

Context in the code you'll write

When you build applications in Module 3, context stops being a chat nuisance and becomes an engineering budget. An agent that appends every tool result to its history will blow the window — and the bill — within a day. Real systems manage context deliberately: trim old messages, summarize completed steps, store full outputs in a database and pass only summaries forward. When you hear terms like 'memory management' or 'context engineering' in job interviews, this is what they mean. You'll implement exactly this pattern in the LangGraph agents of Lesson 11.

Copy-ready prompt

Summarize our conversation so far into a compact handoff block: decisions made, constraints, open questions, and next steps. Keep it under 150 words so I can paste it into a fresh chat.

Here is a long document: [PASTE DOCUMENT]. Give me: (1) a 5-sentence executive summary, (2) a list of the 5 most important facts with where they appear, (3) any contradictions you noticed. Then tell me roughly how many tokens you think it used.

I'm designing an AI app where users upload hundreds of documents. Explain lost-in-the-middle and why I should use retrieval instead of pasting everything, in words a product manager would understand.

💪 Exercise

Take one long, messy chat you've had with an AI this week. Ask the model to summarize it into a handoff block, start a fresh chat with that block, and verify the new chat is as smart as the old one at its best. Write down 2-3 sentences on what you noticed — this is context management, the first real engineering skill of this course.
