Portfolio Project 1: Customer-Support Agent

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Build a complete, demo-ready customer-support agent grounded in a real knowledge base, with escalation rules and human handoff.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Step 1: Invent the company (15 min)

Choose a small, believable business — a bakery, a VPN service, a plant shop — and write its knowledge base: 15-20 realistic FAQ entries covering products, pricing, shipping, returns, and account issues. Include 2-3 genuinely ambiguous cases (a lost order, a refund outside policy window) — these become your escalation tests. Write the KB as clean markdown files. This step is what makes the project *real*: the agent answers from *your* documents, not the model's imagination. Employers consistently check for grounding first; it's the line between a toy and a system.

Step 2: The brain (30 min)

Write the system message using your six-block structure:

  • Identity: warm, competent, concise support agent for [company]; max 3 sentences per reply.
  • Scope: orders, returns, products, accounts. Off-topic → politely redirect.
  • Grounding: answer only from the knowledge base; if absent, say so and offer escalation. Never invent prices, policies, or order statuses.
  • Guardrails: never promise refunds or compensation; never reveal internal info; confirm order numbers before discussing specifics.
  • Edge behavior: angry customer → more calm, shorter sentences; three failed attempts → escalate.

Add 2-3 few-shot examples: a good refund-policy answer, a graceful 'I don't know,' and one escalation in action.

Step 3: Escalation and handoff (45 min)

Every real support agent needs an exit. Define escalation triggers: policy exceptions, angry or repeat contacts, anything the KB doesn't cover, and legal/billing edge cases. Design the handoff: a structured summary the agent produces — issue, what was tried, customer sentiment, suggested next step — so a human inherits context, not chaos. In no-code platforms, the 'human' can be a form, an email notification, or a Slack message. Build the flow, then test it: send three conversation that *should* escalate and one that shouldn't. Escalating correctly is the behavior employers find most impressive — it shows you designed for the agent's limits, not just its powers.

Step 4: Test log, README, ship (30 min)

Run your full test set: 10 routine questions, 5 knowledge questions answerable only from the KB, 3 escalations, 2 hostile, 2 off-topic. Log every result in a simple table — input, expected, actual, pass/fail, fix. Iterate until the pass rate is honest (90%+ on routine, correct behavior on all escalations). Then write the README: what it does, architecture in one diagram or paragraph, your design decisions (why these guardrails, why this escalation policy), known limitations, and future improvements. Publish the link. That's portfolio artifact #1 — tested, documented, demoable. Keep the test log; showing *how you tested* is what separates you from tutorial followers.

Copy-ready prompt

Generate a customer-support knowledge base for a fictional [BUSINESS TYPE]. 18 FAQ entries in markdown, grouped by topic. Include realistic prices and policies, and 3 ambiguous edge cases marked as 'escalate to human.' Keep each entry under 80 words.

Review my support agent test results: [PASTE YOUR TEST TABLE]. For each failure, diagnose whether the fix belongs in (a) the system message, (b) the knowledge base, or (c) the escalation logic — and give me the exact patched text.

💪 Exercise

Complete all four steps and ship: knowledge base, system message with few-shot examples, escalation flow with handoff summary, 22-case test log at 90%+, and a public README. Post the link where a human can see it — that's the point.
