Take-Home Assignments — and How to Crush Them

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Handle AI-role take-home tests professionally — scoping, building, documenting, and presenting so evaluators can't help but be impressed.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Reading the brief: what's really tested

Take-homes almost never test raw skill alone — they test judgment under a deadline. Decode the brief before touching a tool:

  • The explicit ask: build X with Y constraints. Underline the constraints — they're the grading rubric.
  • The implicit tests: usually three — can you scope realistically? do you document decisions? does it actually run?
  • The realistic effort: ask! 'How many hours do you expect candidates to spend?' is a senior-sounding question, not a weak one. If they say 3 hours, submitting 20 hours of work signals you can't scope — a real failure mode.

Then decide your intentional scope: what you'll build, what you'll stub, what you'll list as 'given more time.' Evaluators love a visible future-work.md more than an overbuilt mess.

The build: your Module 4 system, on a clock

This is why Module 4 existed — take-homes are typically mini versions of your portfolio: a support agent, a RAG demo, a pipeline. Run your known-good loop, timeboxed:

  • Hour 1: skeleton and the riskiest part first — if the core won't work, you want to know in hour one, not hour nine.
  • Middle hours: build the smallest complete version that satisfies every explicit requirement. Complete-but-small beats impressive-but-broken, every time.
  • Final third: testing (your test-set habit — even 10 cases puts you ahead of most candidates) and the README.

Non-negotiable: it must run on their machine. Include exact setup steps, a working demo link, and zero 'it worked on my machine' energy. That sentence has ended candidacies.

The README is half your grade

Evaluators read the README before or instead of the code. Use your proven structure:

  • Problem and approach in three sentences, human-readable.
  • What I built / what I deliberately didn't — your scope decisions, stated proudly.
  • How to run it — numbered, tested on a clean machine (or a live link).
  • Design decisions — 'I chose X over Y because Z.' This is where judgment becomes visible and gradeable.
  • Testing — your test set and results table.
  • Given more time — 3 bullets. Shows vision plus discipline.

This is the exact README you've written four times. The take-home rewards exactly the habits you now have. Screenshots and a 60-90 second demo video complete the package — evaluators who feel the demo *work* in two minutes are already on your side.

Presenting it — and protecting yourself

Many processes end with a walkthrough call. Structure it like your Lesson 22 project tour: problem, approach, one decision story, demo, limits, future work — 5-7 minutes, then invite questions. Expect 'why didn't you…' questions: they test composure, not perfection; 'I considered it and deprioritized it because of the time budget — here's where it would slot in' is a *great* answer.

And know your rights: a take-home shouldn't exceed roughly 4-8 hours or solve a company's *current real business problem unpaid. If it smells like free work — real data, production scope, no compensation — it's fair to ask about it, or walk. Companies that respect candidates design respectful take-homes; the assignment is often your first look at how they actually operate.

Copy-ready prompt

Here is a take-home brief: [PASTE BRIEF]. Give me: (1) what's likely being tested beyond the explicit ask, (2) a ruthless scope plan for a [X]-hour budget — build / stub / skip, (3) the 3 risks that could sink it and how to de-risk each early, (4) 5 clarifying questions worth asking before starting.

Review my take-home README like the evaluator: [PASTE README]. Where would I lose points? What's missing? What sentence should I add? Do not rewrite it — give me the fix list.

💪 Exercise

Do a timed drill: find a realistic take-home brief (or generate one with AI: 'write a 4-hour take-home for an AI Automation Specialist role'), then execute the full lifecycle in one sitting — scoped build, test set, README, 60-second demo video. Review: where did the time actually go? Fix your process while it's cheap.
