Cover Letters That Don't Suck

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Write short, specific, human cover letters that get read — using AI for speed and yourself for the parts that matter.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Why most cover letters die

The typical fate: skimmed in 15 seconds by someone who has 200 applications to go. What kills them: generic openers ('I am writing to express my interest…' — instant delete), AI-mush voice (the recruiter has read forty identical 'I am passionate about leveraging cutting-edge AI' letters this week and is professionally numb to it), all claims, no evidence ('detail-oriented team player'), and all about you instead of what you'll do for them. The fix is the same as every lesson in this course: specificity beats polish. One real detail about *their* company is worth a page of perfect adjectives about you. And under 200 words — respect for the skim is itself a signal.

The four-paragraph skeleton

A letter that works, every time:

1. The hook (2 sentences): the specific reason *this* company — something from their product, a post their team wrote, a real problem they have. Proves you did homework; no template smell.

2. The match (3-4 sentences): your most relevant 1-2 qualifications *with evidence* — 'I built a support agent handling 200 weekly emails (live demo: link).' Portfolio evidence > adjectives.

3. The value (2 sentences): what you'll do in the first months — 'I'd start by mapping your support flow and piloting an agent like this one.'

4. The ask (1 sentence): a direct, confident close — 'I'd love 15 minutes to show you the demo.'

Under 200 words, first person, sounds like you.

The 10-minute tailoring ritual

Keep a master letter (your best generic version) and a repeatable tailoring loop per application:

1. Collect 3 facts: why this company, what this role owns, one thing they publicly struggle with or celebrate.

2. Fill paragraph 1 with the *real* reason — a specific product detail, their engineering blog, the job description's own phrasing.

3. Swap paragraph 2's evidence to the project most relevant to *this* role (support agent for support-heavy roles, RAG analyzer for knowledge roles, pipeline for marketing ops).

4. Read it aloud — kill any sentence you'd never say.

AI accelerates steps 1-3 (research, drafting, matching) — but the final read-aloud edit is yours, non-negotiable. That edit is what separates your letter from the mush pile.

The rules of honest AI use

Using AI to draft is smart; submitting unedited AI output is malpractice — recruiters in 2026 spot it instantly and it reads as 'doesn't care.' Your guardrails: AI may draft, research, and tailor; you must supply the specific hooks, verify every claim, and do the final voice edit. Never let the AI invent facts, and never paste a letter that would fit any other company — the interchangeability test: if you could swap the company name and nothing breaks, it's not done. Also: some applications genuinely don't need a letter — spend your energy where a human reads them: smaller companies, referred applications, and roles where the posting clearly wants one. Strategy is part of the craft.

Copy-ready prompt

Here is a job description and the company's website text: [PASTE BOTH]. Give me: (1) 5 specific, real hooks I could open a cover letter with — actual details about this company, no flattery, (2) which of my projects best matches this role and why. My projects: [1-LINE EACH].

Review this cover letter like a tired recruiter with 200 to go: [PASTE LETTER]. Would you keep reading? Kill every cliché, flag every sentence that could apply to any company, and mark the one strongest line. Do not rewrite.

💪 Exercise

Write a real letter using the four-paragraph skeleton for a real job posting — under 200 words, one company-specific hook, one piece of portfolio evidence with a link, and your final read-aloud edit. Run it through the interchangeability test before you call it done.
