Cover Letters That Don't Suck

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Write short, specific cover letters that get read — a repeatable formula built on the company's actual problem, your shipped proof, and zero clichés.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Why most letters die

Symptoms of the dead letter: 'I am writing to express my interest…' · 'I am a highly motivated self-starter…' · 'I believe my skills would be a great fit…' — all about you, none about them, nothing checkable. The reader (a hiring manager with 40 letters and 6 minutes) is asking exactly one question: *can this person do the specific thing we need, and is it worth 30 minutes to find out?* A good letter answers it in the first three lines with a concrete claim plus a link. Your unfair advantage after Module 4: most juniors write 'passionate about AI'; you write 'here are four deployed systems — try one.'

The formula

Five short paragraphs, 150–200 words total:

1. The hook — their problem, in their words. From the posting and 10 minutes of research: 'You're hiring an engineer to scale [Company]'s support automation beyond scripted flows.'

2. The claim. 'I build and deploy exactly those systems: four shipped LLM projects — agents, RAG, MCP tools — each with public demos.'

3. The proof, one best match. Two sentences on the project that maps to their need, with the link and a number: '30-case eval suite, 88% faithfulness.'

4. The specifics. Their stack where it overlaps yours: LangGraph, RAG, FastAPI, Docker.

5. The ask. 'Happy to walk through the demo in 15 minutes.'

No 'to whom it may concern' — find a name on LinkedIn. Attach nothing; link everything.

Research: the 10 minutes that double callbacks

Before writing, answer three questions: What does this company actually build or automate? (product pages, engineering blog, recent launches.) What does the posting repeat? — words appearing three or more times are the real job. Who reads it? — a recruiter screens for keywords, an engineering manager for judgment, so include one of each: the keyword line ('Python, LangGraph, RAG') and a judgment line ('chose evals-in-CI over chasing a bigger model because measured beats guessed'). If the company is Israeli or US-based, note the language norms — direct, specific, no formality theater. Ten minutes of research shows up as specificity, and specificity reads as competence.

The master letter and the short versions

Write one master letter for your top target role, then adapt per application by swapping paragraphs 1, 3, and 4 — five minutes each, keep a /letters folder with one file per company so you can track what you sent. For LinkedIn messages and email intros, compress the formula to three sentences: hook on their problem → your claim + best link → the ask. Example: 'Saw you're scaling agentic support at [Company] — I've shipped a deployed LangGraph support agent with evals (link). Worth 15 minutes?' Same spine, shorter bones. Never send a letter a model wrote unedited — recruiters can smell a template, and one hallucinated claim about their company ends the conversation.
