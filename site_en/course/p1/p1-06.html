Pictures In, Answers Out: Image Prompts in Daily Life

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Use photos as prompts — fridge contents to meal plans, screenshots to explanations, receipts to spending breakdowns.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Fridge → dinner

Open the fridge, take one honest photo of the shelves, and send it with: 'Here's my fridge. Plan 3 dinners from what's visible, plus a short shopping list for the gaps. 30 minutes max per dish, family of four.' The model reads labels, spots the half-empty jar of olives, notices the chicken that should probably be used first, and builds around it. Works for pantry shelves too. Two tricks: take the photo in decent light, and mention hard constraints ('one kid hates mushrooms,' 'kosher kitchen'). It's not clairvoyant — it just sees better than you do on a Tuesday evening.

Screenshot → explanation

Any screen you don't understand becomes a question. A settings page you're scared to touch, a bank statement line you don't recognize, a homework app screenshot, an error dialog, a confusing electric bill. Screenshot it, paste it in, ask: 'Explain what this is saying in plain English, and tell me what to do, if anything.' The pattern that makes it work: say what you *want* — 'I want to know if I'm being charged extra,' not just 'what is this?' For homework screenshots the golden prompt is: 'Don't give the answer — explain the question to my kid so they can solve it.' Lesson 11 expands on that.

Receipt → breakdown

Photograph a supermarket receipt and ask: 'Categorize this receipt: food, household, treats, waste. What did we overpay on? What's roughly our cost per week at this rate?' Do it three weeks in a row and you have a real picture of your grocery spending — no app, no spreadsheet, no data entry. It also handles: warranty text, medication package inserts ('explain this in plain language — and remind me this isn't medical advice'), appliance model plates ('what is this machine, and what's a common problem with it?'), and kids' artwork ('what's a craft we can do with this as inspiration?'). Camera in, answers out.

Photos as context, not just questions

The real upgrade: photos as *context* for bigger questions. Photograph the pile of stuff on your desk and ask for a filing system. Photograph your kid's overflowing closet and ask for a decluttering plan they'll actually follow. Photograph the parking sign that makes no sense. One rule keeps this safe and useful: what's in the frame is what the model sees — crop out the sensitive stuff (full ID numbers, credit cards, other people's kids) before sending. Lesson 19 covers the details, but the habit starts here: look at the photo before you send it, the same way you'd look before posting it anywhere.

Copy-ready prompt

Here's a photo of my open fridge. Plan 3 dinners from what's visible, plus a short shopping list for missing staples. Max 30 minutes per dish, family of [N]. Constraints: [diet, dislikes, allergies].

[Attach screenshot] Explain what this screen/error/bill is saying in plain English. I specifically want to know: [your actual worry]. Tell me what to do, if anything.

[Attach receipt] Categorize everything on this receipt (food / household / treats / impulse buys). What did we overpay on, and what's our rough weekly grocery rate if this is typical?

💪 Exercise

Do all three photo prompts today: fridge photo → dinner plan, one confusing screenshot → plain-English explanation, one receipt → categorized breakdown. Keep the receipt analysis — you'll reuse it in lesson 10.
