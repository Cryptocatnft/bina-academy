Capstone: Your Personal AI System

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Assemble everything from the course into one coherent, documented personal AI system — and leave with a working setup you'll actually keep.
Alex · Play the lecturer

Taking inventory

First, see what you've actually built — most people don't realize how much they're running. One prompt, and we write it all down: 'Help me document my current AI system: my main tool and its customizations (instructions, projects, GPTs), my scheduled tasks, my sidekick tools and what I use each for, my recurring routines (weekly planning, budget check-in, email batching), and the prompts I reuse. Interview me briefly, then produce a one-page summary.' This document — your system's README — becomes the single source of truth. You'll be surprised at the list. Students always are. That's not luck; that's twenty lessons of small, stacking habits.

The one-page system

Structure the page around your week, not around features. Daily (automatic): the morning brief, running on its own. Weekly (10 minutes): Sunday planning ritual, email batch. Monthly (15 minutes): budget pulse, scheduled-prompt audit, privacy check. The map: one line per tool — 'ChatGPT: main, everything, has my instructions + Family HQ project / Perplexity: anything current / Claude: writing that matters.' The rules: the privacy rules from lesson 19, the 'AI drafts, I decide' rule, the 'if it's been in my head more than a day, it gets two minutes today' rule. Print it, pin it, share it with your household. A system you can point at beats one you carry in your head.

Stress-testing and simplifying

Every system needs a shakedown. Run the audit prompt: 'Look at my one-page system. What's fragile? What am I probably not using? What should I cut, and what one thing should I add?' Common findings: too many scheduled tasks (cut to what you actually read), a project that never gets opened (archive it), Custom Instructions that are stale (update them), a GPT with a job too broad (narrow it). The best systems are small. If something hasn't earned its keep in a month, retire it without guilt — the whole point of automation is that firing it is as easy as building it was. Keep what works, cut what doesn't, and be honest about which is which.

What's next

Where do you go from here? Maintenance mode: the quarterly toolbox test from lesson 18, the monthly audits, the living README. And curiosity mode: when a new feature lands — a smarter model, a new voice, whatever comes next — you're now the person in your family and workplace who tries it first and knows exactly how to evaluate it: test prompt, honest comparison, keep or skip. That's the graduation gift: not a bag of tricks, but a method. You started this course asking 'what do I type?' and you're ending it with a system. On behalf of everyone here at BINA — the AI that built the course, the AI that taught it, and me — congratulations. Now go make dinner. From a fridge photo, obviously.

Copy-ready prompt

Interview me briefly and then document my current AI system: main tool and customizations, sidekick tools and their jobs, scheduled tasks, recurring routines, and my reused prompts. Produce a one-page README of my system, organized around my week (daily / weekly / monthly).

System audit: 'Review my README. What's fragile, what am I probably not using, what should I cut? What one addition would pay off most? Be honest, not encouraging.'

Maintenance calendar: 'Build my quarterly AI maintenance checklist: toolbox test with my test prompts, scheduled-task audit, Custom Instructions refresh, privacy settings check. One page, once a season.'

💪 Exercise

Build your README today: run the interview prompt, produce the one-page system doc, cut one thing that isn't earning its keep, and schedule your first quarterly audit in your actual calendar. That doc — plus the habit of keeping it current — is your diploma.
