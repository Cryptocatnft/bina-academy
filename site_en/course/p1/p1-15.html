Document Analysis: Contracts, Policies, and the Fine Print

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Upload your real documents — contracts, insurance policies, leases — and extract what matters: obligations, traps, and the questions to ask.
Alex · Play the lecturer

The upload and the first pass

Every major assistant takes PDFs — drag it into the chat, or photograph the paper version (lesson 6). First pass, always: 'Summarize this document in plain English: what is it, what are my main obligations, what are theirs, and when does it end or renew? Under 200 words.' This orientation matters: before hunting for traps, know what the thing even is. Then the trap hunt: 'List the clauses that could hurt me: auto-renewals, cancellation fees, penalties, liability caps, vague language. For each, quote the clause and say what it means in real life.' Vague language is the tell — 'reasonable notice' and 'as determined by the company' are doing a lot of heavy lifting in your life.

The questions that matter

Documents become useful when you interrogate them like the other side would hate. 'If I cancel in month 7, exactly what do I pay?' 'Who's liable if the delivery damages my stuff?' 'What's NOT covered by this insurance policy?' That last one is the sleeper hit — exclusions are where claims go to die, and policies bury them lovingly. Then the practical close: 'I'm signing this [day]. Give me: the 3 questions to ask before signing, the dates I should put in my calendar (renewal, notice deadline), and anything negotiable.' Most contracts are more negotiable than they look, and now you know where.

Comparing and negotiating

Two upgrades that pay off. First, comparison: upload two options — 'Compare these two [policies / contracts / phone plans] as a table: cost, coverage, cancellation, gotchas. If I care most about [your priority], which wins and why?' Second, the negotiation draft (lesson 12 energy): 'Based on this contract, draft a message asking to change [the cancellation notice period]. Polite, cites the clause, offers something in return — prompt payment, longer commitment.' Fine print is negotiable in more places than people think, and the AI reads the whole document so you don't have to — then arms you with the exact clause number to argue about.

The lawyer line

Now the honesty talk, and this one matters: this is analysis, not legal advice. AI is superb at reading, summarizing, comparing, and spotting suspicious clauses. It is not your lawyer, and for the big-stakes documents — buying a home, a business contract, anything with real money or real disputes attached — a human professional reviews before you sign. The sweet spot: walk into the lawyer 90% prepared, with your questions already written and the three clauses you're worried about flagged. Their hour becomes ten minutes of sharp conversation instead of an hour of reading. That's how you use tools like a grown-up.

Copy-ready prompt

Summarize this document in plain English: what is it, my main obligations, their obligations, when it ends or renews. Under 200 words. Then list the clauses that could hurt me (auto-renewals, fees, penalties, liability, vague language) — quote each and translate to real life.

Before I sign: [1] What happens if I cancel in [month]? [2] What's NOT covered / not included? [3] The 3 questions I should ask the other side, the dates for my calendar, and anything negotiable.

Compare these two documents as a table: cost, coverage/benefits, cancellation, gotchas. If my top priority is [priority], which wins and why?

💪 Exercise

Pick one document you signed but never read — a lease, policy, or service agreement — and run the full pass: summary, trap hunt, questions. Put one date it revealed (renewal or notice deadline) into your actual calendar.
