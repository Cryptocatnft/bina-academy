Scheduled Tasks: AI That Works While You Sleep

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Set up recurring, scheduled AI tasks — the morning brief, the weekly review — so your system runs even when you don't ask.
Alex · Play the lecturer

How scheduling works

ChatGPT has Scheduled Tasks / Scheduled Chats, Gemini has scheduled actions in its assistant surfaces — features keep evolving, so look for 'schedule' in your tool's settings or ask it directly: 'can you schedule this to run daily?' The mechanism is simple: a prompt + a schedule ('every weekday at 6:30 AM'). At the appointed time the AI runs your prompt and delivers the result to your chat or notifications. The important mental shift: it's the difference between a colleague you have to summon and one who emails you the morning summary unprompted. The work happens at the edges of your day, without your attention — which is exactly where attention is scarcest.

The starter set

Three scheduled tasks cover most people's lives. The morning brief (weekdays 6:30 AM): 'Today's brief: weather for [city] with kid-relevant notes (rain gear?), my calendar for today in one glance, top 3 priorities from my running list, and one interesting thing worth knowing. Under 150 words, plain text.' The weekly review (Sunday 7 PM): kick off the lesson-9 planning ritual — 'It's planning time. Ask me for this week's brain dump.' The money pulse (monthly): 'It's statement day — remind me to export transactions for our budget check-in.' Start with just the brief. One habit, running quietly, earning trust — then add.

Writing prompts for the future

Scheduled prompts are written for future-you, who may have forgotten the context entirely — so they must be self-contained. Every schedule prompt needs: what it is ('this is my daily brief'), the standing rules (length, tone, format — under 150 words, plain text, no headers), what to include, and what to skip. Test each one manually first: run the prompt a few times in a normal chat, fix the wording until the output is right, and only then schedule it. A prompt that works 'now' plus a clear self-description works 'every morning at 6:30.' One that doesn't will quietly deliver mediocre coffee-table trivia to your phone every single day.

Guardrails

Unattended automation needs maintenance, or it goes stale: a brief that pulls from your calendar needs that calendar connected; a review that assumes life hasn't changed will drift — so revisit your scheduled prompts monthly, same as you'd check a smoke detector. Keep the count low (3-4 max; more becomes noise you swipe away), keep each one's job narrow, and audit what they can see — a scheduled task reading your email or calendar is fine, but check what the permission actually grants (lesson 19 habits again). The test of a good schedule: when it runs, you actually read it. If you've swiped it away five days running, fix it or kill it.

Copy-ready prompt

Morning brief (schedule: weekdays 6:30 AM): 'My daily brief: weather for [city] with kid-relevant notes, my calendar today at a glance, top 3 priorities, one thing worth knowing. Under 150 words, plain text, no headers.'

Weekly review kick-off (schedule: Sunday 7 PM): 'It is weekly planning time. Ask me for this week's brain dump, then run our planning routine: sort, realistic schedule, flag conflicts, protect one evening for me.'

Monthly money pulse (schedule: 1st of the month): 'Statement day reminder: export last month's transactions and run our budget comparison. If I don't respond today, remind me again tomorrow.'

💪 Exercise

Set up one scheduled task today — the morning brief is the classic. Write the prompt, test it twice manually, schedule it, and read it tomorrow morning with your coffee. Note one thing you'd change, and change it.
