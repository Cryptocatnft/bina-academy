Email and Messages on Autopilot

🎯 What you'll be able to do: Turn the worst part of your day — emails and messages — into a five-minute review of drafts the AI wrote in your voice.
Alex · Play the lecturer

The three-line briefing

Every good email draft needs the same three things: the incoming message (paste it), your goal ('decline politely,' 'get a refund,' 'sound firm but nice'), and the relationship ('my kid's teacher,' 'the landlord — I want to stay on good terms'). That's it. The AI writes the draft; you edit the parts that feel off. The magic is that it handles the emotional labor — the softening phrases, the 'hope you're well' padding — while you keep the judgment about what to actually say. Draft in ten seconds, edit in thirty, send with confidence instead of dread.

The hard ones

Where this really earns its keep: the emails you keep not sending. Saying no to a family invitation without a feud. Asking a friend to repay a loan. Complaining to a company — give the AI the facts and let it write the firm-but-fair version: 'Here's my order #, here's what went wrong, here's what I want: a replacement or refund. Polite but firm, one follow-up implied.' Boundary-setting texts — 'I can't make it, no excuses needed' — stop being a forty-minute emotional event. The rule: if it's been in your head more than a day, it gets the two-minute AI treatment today.

Your voice, preloaded

Sick of editing every draft? Teach it your voice once. Paste three emails or texts you actually wrote, and say: 'Here are three messages I wrote. Learn my voice: length, warmth, how I open and close. From now on in this chat, draft in this voice.' Even better, put a permanent version in Custom Instructions (lesson 2): 'Emails: short, warm, direct. No 'I hope this email finds you well.' Sign-offs: just my name.' Suddenly drafts arrive 90% right. Save the fancy vocabulary for nobody — your people recognize your voice, and now the machine speaks it.

Workflow and guardrails

Batch it: twice a day, open the messy pile, run each one through the draft mill, send. Most people cut their email time by half or more — and the avoidance tax drops to zero. Guardrails, though: always read before sending. The model can misread tone, invent a fact ('I'll be there Thursday' — you never said that), or be too apologetic. It drafts; you own the send button. Never paste secrets you wouldn't email a colleague — passwords, full ID numbers, health details — into a chat for a birthday-party RSVP. Judgment stays human. That's not a limitation; that's the design.

Copy-ready prompt

Here's the message I need to reply to: [paste]. My relationship to them: [e.g., kid's teacher / landlord / coworker]. My goal: [e.g., decline politely / get refund / set a boundary]. Draft a reply in my voice — short, warm, direct. No filler.

Learn my voice. Here are three messages I wrote: [paste 3 samples]. From now on in this chat, draft everything in this voice: match length, warmth, and how I open and close.

Write a firm but polite complaint email. Facts: [order #, what went wrong]. What I want: [replacement / refund / fix]. One paragraph of facts, one of the ask, imply I'll follow up if ignored.

💪 Exercise

Pick the three messages you've been avoiding longest. Run each through the three-line briefing, edit lightly, and send at least one today. Note how long the whole batch took versus your usual avoidance cycle.
